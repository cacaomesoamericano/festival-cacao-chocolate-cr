Terroir of Mesoamerican cacao

The opening talk of the festival is broadcast live from Costa Rica with simultaneous translation into English, for chocolate makers, buyers and specialists following the festival from abroad. It presents the taste profiles, chemistry and quality attributes of fine cacao from Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panama, alongside the genetics, history and growing regions behind them.

The session supports the case for Geographical Indications for Mesoamerican cacao, drawing on the Cacaos de Origen Mesoamericano project of FONTAGRO, which treats origin quality as technical, cultural and social at once.

The framework

Geographical Indications for Mesoamerican cacao

The Cacaos de Origen Mesoamericano project, funded by FONTAGRO and led from the Universidad Nacional de Costa Rica, is building that evidence base in Honduras and Costa Rica so that origin quality can be protected and rewarded. The speaker

Dr. Carlos Eduardo Hernández

Universidad Nacional de Costa Rica

Dr. Carlos Eduardo Hernández is an agronomist, graduated from the Universidad Nacional de Costa Rica (UNA, 2001), and holds a master's degree and a PhD in Food Engineering (2004, 2010), both from the National Pingtung University of Science and Technology (NPUST), Taiwan.

He is a professor and researcher at UNA's School of Agricultural Sciences, where he works on research and technology transfer in fermentation systems for the production of differentiated-quality cacao. He currently leads the FONTAGRO project "Geographical Indications for Mesoamerican Cacao", whose initial phases are being carried out in Honduras and Costa Rica.

His work is multidisciplinary: it combines applied optics to determine cacao quality profiles, functional metagenomics and the study of microbial diversity in fermentation, and volatile profile analysis by GC-MS/SPME — research lines that have led to a number of scientific publications. He is an IICCT-certified Level 3 cacao and chocolate taster.

His vision is to promote innovation based on local resources, protect biocultural heritage, and integrate quality analysis as a tool to add value to Mesoamerican cacao and to the people who produce it.
